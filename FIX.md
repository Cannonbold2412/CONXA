## Support can now grant someone a paid plan for free, with an optional expiry date — 2026-08-09
Until now, the only way a customer got Starter or Pro access was by actually paying through checkout — there was no way for us to comp a plan for a sales demo, a support fix, or a trial extension without either a real charge or someone hand-editing the database. Now there's a proper backend switch: give a specific customer Starter, Pro, or Enterprise access directly, and optionally set how long the grant should last — 1 month, 3 months, a year, whatever's needed. When a timed grant runs out, the customer automatically drops back to the free plan on its own; nobody has to remember to go take it away.
While building this we also found and fixed a small pre-existing bug: if a customer published two automations in the same publish action, the system could occasionally get confused about which one was actually older when deciding what to lock during a downgrade — like a filing system that sometimes misfiles same-day paperwork. Automations now keep a precise creation order internally, so the oldest-gets-locked-first rule always picks the right one.
 — 2026-08-09

## Downgrading a plan now locks the extra workflows you built while on the higher plan — 2026-08-09
Earlier today we found that someone could pay for the top-tier "Pro" plan for a month, build 500 automations, then drop to the cheaper "Starter" plan and quietly keep all 500 running forever, even though Starter is only supposed to allow 200. Nothing was reclaiming that extra capacity — a downgrade only stopped you from building *new* ones past the limit, it never touched what you'd already built.
Now it does. Every automation a company builds is tracked on a permanent list, oldest to newest. If a company ever has more automations than their current plan allows, the oldest ones over the limit get automatically locked — not deleted, just paused — while the newest ones (up to the plan's limit) stay live. Locking is one-way reversible: upgrade again and the locked ones reopen automatically, oldest first, no manual work needed on either side. Locked automations also can't be republished with a new version while they're locked, and a company already at its limit can't sneak in a brand-new automation either — both require freeing up room or upgrading. Importantly, this only affects what the SaaS company can manage going forward; any of *their* customers who already had one of these automations installed keep using it uninterrupted, so a plan change on the company's side never breaks something a real end customer is actively relying on.
 — 2026-08-09

## Checked what happens when someone downgrades their plan after using the higher tier for a while — 2026-08-09
We looked into whether someone could pay for the top-tier "Pro" plan for a month or two, use everything it unlocks, then drop back down to a cheaper plan and quietly keep the extra value. Good news: for the things billed by usage — AI credits, premium features, how far a workflow can be distributed — the system checks your *current* plan every single time, so a downgrade takes effect immediately, the same day. Nobody can bank premium usage ahead of a downgrade.
Two things don't have that same protection yet, and we wrote them up rather than silently patching them: the number of computers a company is allowed to build on, and how many teammates can be invited. Computers already behave reasonably — once you're over your new plan's limit, you're blocked from adding more, though anything already registered under the old plan quietly keeps working. Teammate invites have no such check at all right now — a company can invite more people than their plan allows on any plan, not just after a downgrade, because invites go straight to our login provider and never pass through our own servers to be checked. That one needs new plumbing to fix properly (listening for invite events from the login provider and removing anyone who pushes a company over its limit), so it's logged as prioritized follow-up work rather than rushed in.
 — 2026-08-09

## Recompiling a workflow now spends a build credit, not the editing budget — 2026-08-09
If you re-recorded a workflow and then rebuilt it, that rebuild was quietly free against your monthly build allowance and instead ate into your separate editing budget — the same pool meant for small fixes like re-pointing a button after a page redesign. That was backwards: rebuilding a whole workflow is real, expensive work, the same as building it the first time. Now every rebuild, first time or a repeat, spends one build credit, exactly like the first build always did. Fixing or re-targeting a single step by hand still uses the separate editing budget, unchanged.
 — 2026-08-09

## Closed two ways a free trial could quietly turn into free, unlimited distribution — 2026-08-09
Yesterday's change limited a free trial to one computer for building — but that limit stopped at the building step. Two gaps meant a copy of what got built could still travel further than intended. First: the installer file itself had no memory of which computer it came from, so it could be copied and run anywhere, on any number of machines, forever. Second, and more important: even after a company's plan changed, our servers kept sending their workflow updates out to every customer who already had the software installed, with no check at all — like a magazine that keeps mailing new issues to old subscribers even after the subscription was cancelled.
Both are fixed now. A trial installer is locked to the one computer it was built on and simply won't run anywhere else. And our servers now check, every time an update goes out, whether the sending company's current plan is actually allowed to reach outside customers — a free trial gets zero further updates sent to anyone but its own one registered computer, full stop, even if that company was previously on a paid plan and downgraded. As part of this we also loosened one existing rule: the mid-tier "Starter" plan can now reach unlimited customer computers (it was previously capped to internal use only), since its existing monthly limit on how many times a workflow can be rebuilt already keeps its usage in check without a separate machine cap.
 — 2026-08-09

## Rebuilt our pricing and our pitch around one growth story instead of two confusing ones — 2026-08-08
After a demo with a potential customer, we got hard but useful feedback: we couldn't clearly say who our product is for, and our pricing and website were carrying features for two different kinds of customer at once, which made the whole thing feel muddled. We fixed the story first, then rebuilt the pricing and the software underneath it to match. The new story is one ladder, not two products: a free trial lets someone prove the tool works on one computer, a paid plan lets a team run it across their whole organization, and the top plan lets a company ship it out to their own customers, with their own branding. Every price, every limit, and every "you need to upgrade for that" message on the website and in the app now comes from that same ladder, so nothing can drift out of sync with what we actually charge for.

Along the way we removed an old limit on how many products a company could publish (there's no longer a cap), and replaced it with a limit on how many computers a trial account can be used from — a much better way to stop someone from running a free trial forever. We also added the option, for our largest customers, to plug in their own AI provider account instead of using ours, which matters to banks and other companies with strict rules about where their data can go. All of the pricing pages, help pages, and internal engineering guides were rewritten to match, so nobody on the team or in support is working from an old, out-of-date price sheet.
 — 2026-08-08

## Rebuilt the customer dashboard into an operations control center — 2026-08-07
The dashboard customers see after logging in was written for engineers: a wall of counters and lists that told you what had broken, but never whether things were actually going well. It is now organised around the questions a manager or executive actually asks, each with its own page — is the platform healthy, what needs attention right now, which workflows are slipping, is the self-repair working, and what is all of this worth in saved time and money. A single health score sits at the top, and unlike most such scores it shows its own workings: the five things that produced it are listed underneath, so "we dropped four points" immediately becomes "our checks started failing." A live feed shows work as it happens, and everything is clickable down to a single run, step by step.
Two things were deliberately kept honest. The "hours saved" figure needs someone to say how long the task used to take a person — the software has no way of knowing that — so the assumption is printed right next to the number and anyone in charge can edit it, and it is labelled an estimate. Numbers that come purely from real activity are labelled measured, and kept separate. The dashboard also flags problems in plain language ("this new version is failing more often than the one before it — consider rolling back") using fixed rules rather than an AI, so every warning can be traced to a number and clicked through to the proof.
 — 2026-08-07

## Fixed the "free repairs" count being roughly double the real figure — 2026-08-07
While building the new dashboard, a number turned out to be wrong in a way that flattered us. When a workflow repairs itself, it tries the cheap local methods first and only calls on an AI model if those fail. We report how many repairs were handled free of charge — but the old count added up attempts rather than outcomes, so one step that tried two free methods was counted twice, and a step that tried a free method, failed, and then needed a paid AI call was still counted as free. On real data it reported 96 free repairs out of 64 total repairs, which is impossible on the face of it. It now counts what it says: steps that genuinely finished without ever needing a paid AI call. The same figure appears on two different pages, and both now read from one source so they can never disagree.
 — 2026-08-07
